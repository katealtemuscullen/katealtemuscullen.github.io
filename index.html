My research examines the governance and political economy of climate change adaptation, with a focus on water. I take a mixed methods and participatory research approach, integrating field-based qualitative analysis, household surveying, and computational decision-support modeling.

I am currently a Postdoctoral Scholar at the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability, collaborating with Sarah Fletcher in the Department of Civil & Environmental Engineering. I received my PhD in environmental social sciences from the Energy & Resources Group at the University of California, Berkeley.

My work has been supported by NASA ROSES, NSF, Fulbright-Hays, and Fulbright, among others.

Research Portfolio

I study how governance, from community water collectives to global climate institutions, shapes who is able to adapt to climate change, and how. My work sits at the intersection of political economy, institutions, and climate adaptation, with a focus on water. I’m especially interested in how adaptation can either deepen or ease inequalities in who has access to water.

Many of my best research questions come from the people living with these challenges every day. Much of my work is participatory: I partner with community leaders, water managers, and planners to design studies, collect data, and share results in ways that are useful locally. I pair this field-based work, including interviews and household surveys, with computational modeling, so that adaptation planning better reflects how decisions are actually made on the ground.

Making climate adaptation plans politically feasible

Computer models used to plan water and energy systems usually assume that planners will choose whichever option performs best on cost and reliability. In practice, politics, permitting, and institutions shape what actually gets built. At Stanford, I’m developing a method to build political and institutional feasibility directly into these decision-support models. In Santiago, Chile, I draw on interviews with stakeholders and on regulatory and permitting documents to define new feasibility constraints, then build them into a water system planning model to see how they change the options it recommends.

Collaborators: Sarah Fletcher (Stanford Civil & Environmental Engineering), academic partners in Chile, and water utility planners in Santiago.

Community water and megadrought in rural Chile

Central Chile has been in a historic megadrought since 2010. For my PhD at UC Berkeley, I spent twelve months working alongside community leaders and water managers in three rural villages in Chile’s Central Valley that lack formal water access. Together, we carried out 97 interviews and 178 household surveys, and I co-developed a household climate impact assessment with community leaders. This work shows how community-managed water collectives negotiate with the state and the market to keep water flowing, how the design of water governance can leave rural leaders’ priorities out of adaptation planning, and how income and long-standing local ties shape which households are hit hardest. I shared the findings with each community through Spanish-language community reports.

Partners: Community leaders and community-managed water collectives in Viluco, El Llano de Caleu, and Los Maitenes/El Alfalfal. Support: Fulbright-Hays, among others.
Selected work: Papers in review at World Development (2026 AAG Water best student paper award) and npj Climate Action; community reports (2025).

Glacier retreat and the future of water in the Andes

With support from NASA, I designed and led a project on how shrinking glaciers are changing water availability for Santiago. I built a glacio-hydrological model of the city’s watershed and found that glacier meltwater will decline from mid-century onward, likely beyond what the city’s current water management plan can make up for. With colleagues, I’ve also written about what Santiago’s iconic skyline glaciers mean culturally, and what is lost as they retreat.

Selected work: Frontiers in Earth Science (2025); Journal of Hydrology: Regional Studies (2023); Ladera Sur op-ed (2025).

The evolution of global climate governance

Through work at five UN climate conferences, I’ve followed how global climate governance is changing. With colleagues, I’ve examined how bringing water into climate negotiations since COP26 creates trade-offs for the goal of universal drinking water access, and assessed the credibility of net zero targets set by more than 4,000 companies and governments. We’re now writing about how climate governance, finance, and research have kept adaptation and decarbonization in separate silos.

Selected work: Ecology and Society (2025); Climate Policy (2021); Taking Stock report (2021).

Looking Ahead

Climate adaptation and water access in California’s Central Valley

In partnership with rural communities in California’s Central Valley, I’m beginning a project on how intensifying drought-flood cycles and the shift to sustainable groundwater management will shape who has access to water. Following the approach I developed in Chile, I’ll pair participatory fieldwork with long-term local partners and water systems modeling. I’m currently organizing a 2027 American Association of Geographers panel on the topic.

Loss and damage in global climate governance

Some losses from climate change can’t be avoided, and new global institutions are emerging in response. With Hélène Benveniste (Stanford) and colleagues at Columbia, I’ll study how these institutions define and document non-economic losses, and what that means for who can access climate finance. This project builds on partnerships with the Loss & Damage Collaboration that began at COP30.

How adaptive capacity changes over time

Adaptive capacity is usually measured at a single moment. I plan to return to the same Chilean communities in 2029 and 2034 to repeat my household survey, tracing how households’ and communities’ ability to adapt grows or erodes over a decade of drought, and what that means for supporting locally led adaptation.

I’m always glad to connect with community partners, collaborators, and students interested in this work.
